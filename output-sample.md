# PC Build Pitfalls to Avoid in 2026

> Output example — this article is compiled from the wiki entries of this sample knowledge base (see `1_Wiki/`). It demonstrates the **Output** stage of the pipeline: knowledge → content.

Every week, the same three questions come up in customer chats. Here are the answers, backed by the rules this studio uses on every build.

## 1. Your budget split is probably wrong

The fastest machine is not the one with the biggest number on the box. Split the budget like this:

- **CPU + GPU: 50-60%** — they define the performance class.
- **Motherboard + RAM + storage: 20-25%** — buy for the platform, not the brand.
- **PSU: ~10%, never cut here.** An unstable power supply silently degrades everything and is the most returned part.
- **Case + cooling: the rest.** The only place looks justify spending.

See [[pc-budget-allocation]] for the full rule.

## 2. The bottleneck is what you feel

A machine is only as fast as its slowest link. A weak CPU starves a strong GPU in games; too little RAM bottlenecks everything regardless of the CPU. When comparing configs, compare the *combination*, not the headline spec — see [[pc-build-bottleneck]].

## 3. Intel vs AMD: decide by budget, not by brand

- Mid-budget gaming: AMD usually wins on value.
- Non-technical buyers: Intel's brand recognition makes them feel safer — which is a real factor in the sale.
- Workstations: decide by core count and software benchmarks.

The comparison table lives in [[intel-vs-amd-cpu]].

## 4. Prebuilt is not better — it's just safer-feeling

Custom builds win on price and balance above ~5000. What makes custom feel risky is the unknown — which is why a good seller explains the *why* behind every config ([[prebuilt-vs-custom-pc]], [[customer-conversion-flow]]).

## 5. Verify with numbers

After the build, run the three free tools in [[pc-benchmark-tools]] — Cinebench, 3DMark, CrystalDiskMark — and compare against the expected scores. Numbers close the sale and back the after-sales promise.
